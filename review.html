What the simulator covers

This CNC Simulator Pro review starts with scope, because breadth is the program's main selling point. It simulates mills from 2 to 4 axes, lathes, laser, plasma and waterjet cutters, and 3D printers. The vendor counts more than 40 machines in five categories, from large industrial models to small hobby CNCs, and a separate online catalog lists each machine with pictures and axis limits.

You run a program on a virtual controller and watch material come off a virtual workpiece. Collision checks warn when the tool or holder touches something it should not. The graphics aim for realism in materials and effects, which helps students connect what they see on screen with what happens at a real machine.

Everything here comes from the vendor's own pages; this site has not run timed tests or benchmarks. To judge speed and feel on your hardware, the free trial is the honest test.

Training material and support

The support site is better stocked than many small vendors manage. It has an online help manual for version 4 and a separate one for version 3.2, the codes quick reference, and five free web tools: setup program generators for milling and turning, workpiece builders for lathe and mill, and a workpiece viewer. CNC Academy adds tutorials and video lessons.

Help beyond the manual runs through a support portal with FAQs, known issues and a ticket form. The vendor does not publish response times, so plan for the help desk being a small team rather than a round the clock service.

Limits worth knowing

The constraints are the ones the vendor states itself, and they decide whether the program fits you.

  • Windows 10 or later in 64 bit only; there is no macOS or Linux installer, which the system requirements page covers in detail.
  • Mills go to four axes; five axis work is outside the library.
  • Version 4 needs a reasonably modern graphics card; weak PCs are pointed to legacy 3.2, which is no longer updated.
  • The perpetual licence does not include free updates, so the annual plan is the only way to stay current without paying again.
  • New releases can trigger a Windows SmartScreen warning until the build gains reputation.

Who should pick something else

If you only need to preview 3 axis router programs, a free open source tool may do the job; the comparison with CAMotics shows what you give up. If you run a production shop with expensive five axis machines, industrial verifiers built around exact machine models are a better match, and the list of alternatives names several. For everyone in between, the pricing page explains which licence makes sense.

Frequently asked questions

Is CNC Simulator Pro good for beginners?

Yes, beginners are a main audience. It ships with tutorials and example programs, and the vendor's site adds a codes quick reference and video lessons. Mistakes cost nothing because the machine is virtual.

Can it simulate five axis machining?

No. The mills in its library run from 2 to 4 axes. For five axis verification, industrial tools such as VERICUT or NCSIMUL are the usual choice.

Does the simulator include CAM?

Yes. SimCam is built in and offers 2D and 3D CAD/CAM tools. You can draw a part, create a toolpath and simulate it without a separate CAM program.

Has this site benchmarked the program?

No. The review is based on the vendor's published features, requirements and licence terms. The trial is the best way to check speed on your own hardware.

What is the main drawback?

For most buyers it is the platform: the program needs 64 bit Windows 10 or later and a fairly modern graphics card. Buyers of the perpetual licence should also note that updates are not included for free.
